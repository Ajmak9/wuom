## 1. Overview

The **Soil Interface** incorporates the organization of soil as the living foundation of territorial habitability into situated orientation.

It does not interpret soil as an inert surface.

It does not reduce soil to:

- land availability;
- agricultural substrate;
- property;
- construction support;
- green surface;
- a technical soil classification.

It interprets soil as the **functional living base through which territorial processes are sustained, transformed, and connected**.

> **Soil is not only where territorial processes occur.**
>
> **It is an active part of what allows the territory to continue functioning.**

---

## 2. Foundational Question

> **How can the organization of soil be incorporated into situated orientation in order to understand when a territory preserves or loses its capacity to sustain life?**

This question defines the scope of the interface.

The Soil Interface does not primarily ask:

> What type of soil is present?

It asks:

> **What can this soil still do?**

The permanent operational question is:

> **Is this soil still functioning as living support for the territory?**

---

## 3. Organizing Center

The organizing center of the interface is:

> **The organization of soil as the living support of territorial habitability.**

This center distinguishes Soil from the other BIOIA/WUOM interfaces:

- **Water** — hydroterritorial dynamics;
- **Biodiversity** — ecological organization;
- **Community** — community organization;
- **Time** — temporal coherence;
- **Temperature** — thermal habitability of the territory;
- **Connectivity** — organization of territorial connections;
- **Energy** — organization of territorial energy flows.

Soil does not replace any of them.

It supports them from below while preserving its own domain, identity, and limits.

---

## 4. Why This Interface Emerges

BIOIA/WUOM interfaces do not appear to complete a list.

They appear when repeated territorial evidence reveals a dimension that requires its own reading organ.

Soil has appeared throughout the development of the system in relation to:

- water infiltration;
- moisture retention;
- resistance of trees and vegetation to heat;
- thermal regulation;
- biodiversity;
- agriculture;
- ecological connectivity;
- urban and rural habitability;
- carbon storage;
- biological activity;
- regeneration and recovery.

Soil rarely appears as the visible protagonist.

However, almost no territorial process can remain functional without it.

The interface emerges because many existing organs depended on a living base that had not yet been formally incorporated as its own interface.

## 5. Identity

The Soil Interface is a first-order organ within BIOIA/WUOM.

It reads soil as:

- living infrastructure;
- functional support;
- biological habitat;
- hydrological matrix;
- thermal regulator;
- energy store and transformer;
- territorial memory;
- material interface among systems.

Its function is to interpret when soil:

- preserves essential functions;
- begins to lose them;
- enters degradation;
- maintains resilience;
- shows recovery;
- requires protection;
- can regenerate through situated action and return.

The interface does not seek to classify soil.

It seeks to interpret **soil functionality within the territory**.

---

## 6. Purpose

The purpose of the Soil Interface is to understand how soil sustains territorial habitability and how its functions evolve under pressure, care, degradation, or regeneration.

The interface makes it possible to:

- read soil as living infrastructure;
- distinguish visible soil from functional soil;
- interpret infiltration and water retention;
- recognize soil structure and stability;
- observe organic matter and biological activity;
- identify compaction, erosion, sealing, and functional loss;
- relate soil to water, temperature, biodiversity, energy, connectivity, community, and time;
- understand when soil preserves or loses capacity to sustain life;
- orient situated and proportional decisions;
- verify interpretations through territorial return.

The interface does not seek to add more green surface.

It seeks to determine whether the territory retains the ability to function as a living territory.

## 7. Essential Soil Functions

The Soil Interface interprets the functional organization of soil through a set of essential territorial processes.

### 7.1 Water Infiltration and Retention

Functional soil allows water to:

- enter;
- circulate;
- remain available;
- reduce runoff;
- support vegetation;
- sustain ecological processes.

Soil is therefore not simply wet or dry surface.

It is a matrix of hydrological regulation.

### 7.2 Carbon Storage

Soil stores carbon through:

- organic matter;
- roots;
- microorganisms;
- humus;
- biological processes.

Carbon storage is interpreted as part of soil function, not as an isolated metric.

### 7.3 Nutrient Cycling

Functional soil sustains:

- decomposition;
- mineralization;
- biological exchange;
- plant nutrition;
- renewal of organic matter.

The interface interprets nutrient cycling as a living territorial process.

### 7.4 Support for Biodiversity

Soil sustains:

- roots;
- fungi;
- microorganisms;
- soil fauna;
- vegetation;
- habitats;
- ecological relationships.

Soil is not only the support of plants.

It is itself a living habitat.

### 7.5 Thermal Regulation

Soil influences:

- heat accumulation;
- thermal dissipation;
- moisture availability;
- vegetation and shade;
- nighttime recovery;
- differences between living soil and sealed surface.

Soil contributes to thermal habitability without replacing the Temperature Interface.

### 7.6 Resilience and Recovery

Functional soil supports:

- resistance to pressure;
- regeneration after disturbance;
- recovery of vegetation;
- restoration of biological activity;
- continuity of water and nutrient cycles.

Resilience is interpreted as a process rather than a static condition.

## 8. Visible Soil and Functional Soil

The architecture distinguishes between two different conditions.

### Visible Soil

Physical presence of earth or an unbuilt surface.

### Functional Soil

Soil capable of:

- infiltrating water;
- retaining moisture;
- supporting roots;
- hosting biological life;
- regulating temperature;
- maintaining structure;
- cycling nutrients;
- sustaining biodiversity;
- supporting vegetation;
- recovering after disturbance.

A territory may retain visible soil while losing part of its functionality.

A city may still have soil beneath buildings, asphalt, and pavement while having lost operational contact with its functions.

> **The city did not lose the soil.**
>
> **It covered it.**

The interface therefore does not confuse the physical presence of soil with the continuity of soil functions.

## 12. Emerging Architectural Observations

### 12.1 Soil as Living Infrastructure

Soil is a living infrastructure of the territory.

It is not built infrastructure.

It is biological, hydrological, thermal, ecological, and material infrastructure.

It sustains processes that may remain invisible but condition territorial habitability.

### 12.2 Soil as Territorial Memory

Soil records:

- past uses;
- erosion;
- compaction;
- loss of organic matter;
- water accumulation;
- presence or absence of biological life;
- continuity or interruption of territorial processes.

Soil sustains the present while retaining traces of past territorial management.

### 12.3 Soil as an Interface Between Systems

Soil connects:

- atmosphere;
- water;
- vegetation;
- fauna;
- microorganisms;
- agriculture;
- cities;
- energy;
- communities.

It is not an isolated layer.

It is a material interface among multiple systems.

### 12.4 Soil as a Limit of Habitability

When soil loses functionality, the territory loses capacity to:

- absorb water;
- resist heat;
- sustain vegetation;
- support biodiversity;
- reduce erosion;
- maintain agricultural continuity;
- recover balance.

Soil degradation does not always produce an immediate alarm.

It may slowly alter the capacity of the territory to remain habitable.

## 13. Operational Flow

The Soil Interface follows the common BIOIA/WUOM sequence:

```text
Observe
        ↓
Interpret
        ↓
Relate
        ↓
Orient
        ↓
Return
```

### 13.1 Observe

Observation may include:

- infiltration;
- moisture;
- vegetation cover;
- erosion;
- compaction;
- organic matter;
- biological activity;
- associated vegetation;
- terrain stability;
- ecological continuity.

Observation is the basis of the reading.

### 13.2 Interpret

The interface asks:

- Does the soil maintain functional capacity?
- Which functions remain active?
- Which functions are weakening?
- Are there signs of degradation?
- Are there signs of recovery?
- Which functions require protection?
- Which functions may be restored?

Interpretation organizes evidence without replacing it.

### 13.3 Relate

Soil is interpreted in relation to:

- Water;
- Temperature;
- Biodiversity;
- Energy;
- Connectivity;
- Community;
- Time.

The same soil alteration may affect several interfaces simultaneously.

### 13.4 Orient

Orientation may indicate:

- conserve;
- protect;
- restore;
- reduce pressure;
- observe in greater detail;
- maintain monitoring;
- allow recovery;
- avoid intervention where the system remains functional.

Orientation proposes a direction.

It does not impose a universal solution.

### 13.5 Return

Every orientation returns to the territory through new observation.

Return makes it possible to verify:

- whether functions remain active;
- whether improvements appear;
- whether new problems emerge;
- whether the orientation requires adjustment;
- whether recovery is stable or temporary.

Without return, there is no territorial learning.

## 17. Operational Criteria

The interface operates according to eight criteria.

### 17.1 Functionality

Soil is interpreted according to the functions it maintains.

The central question is:

> **Is it still functioning as living soil?**

### 17.2 Continuity

Soil must be read as a process rather than an isolated snapshot.

Continuity makes it possible to distinguish among:

- stability;
- degradation;
- recovery;
- regeneration.

### 17.3 Relationship

Soil is never interpreted in isolation.

Every reading must relate it to the other relevant interfaces.

### 17.4 Scale

Observation may occur locally.

Interpretation must incorporate the territorial context.

The same signal may acquire different meanings at different scales.

### 17.5 Evidence

Every orientation must be supported by observable signals.

Hypotheses are part of the process.

Claims require territorial return.

### 17.6 Neutrality

The reading does not depend on:

- personal preferences;
- ideological positions;
- partisan interests;
- prior expectations.

The permanent reference is the territory.

### 17.7 Uncertainty

Every reading includes uncertainty.

When evidence is insufficient:

- observation continues;
- conclusions remain proportional;
- interpretation remains open;
- return guides revision.

### 17.8 Reversibility

Every orientation must be open to revision.

When return contradicts a previous interpretation, the reading changes.

Not the territory.

### Water

Soil regulates:

- infiltration;
- retention;
- moisture;
- runoff;
- erosion.

Without functional soil, water is lost more rapidly or becomes more erosive.

### Temperature

Soil influences:

- heat accumulation;
- thermal dissipation;
- available moisture;
- vegetation and shade;
- nighttime recovery.

Thermal habitability depends partly on soil functionality.

### Biodiversity

Soil supports:

- roots;
- fungi;
- microorganisms;
- soil fauna;
- vegetation cover;
- habitats.

Functional biodiversity requires sufficient soil support.

### Energy

Soil participates in:

- storage of biological energy;
- transformation of organic matter;
- metabolic cycles;
- biomass production;
- exchange among vegetation, water, and atmosphere.

### Connectivity

Soil enables:

- ecological continuity;
- biological corridors;
- territorial permeability;
- connections among water, vegetation, and fauna;
- continuity among urban, rural, and natural areas.

- agriculture;
- habitability;
- spaces of life;
- territorial health;
- continuity of practices;
- rootedness.

- isolated erosion;
- accumulated degradation;
- progressive regeneration;
- difficult or irreversible loss.

## 21. Foundational Territorial Origin

The Soil Interface does not emerge from abstraction alone.

It is grounded in repeated territorial observations such as:

- long-term care of an olive grove without herbicides;
- improvement in tree resistance after abundant rainfall;
- persistent heat revealing the importance of soil moisture;
- urban territories where soil disappears beneath asphalt and concrete;
- observation of the relationship among trees, water, and habitability;
- comparison between living soil and sealed surface.

The interface existed first in territorial experience.

It later acquired language, structure, and method.
