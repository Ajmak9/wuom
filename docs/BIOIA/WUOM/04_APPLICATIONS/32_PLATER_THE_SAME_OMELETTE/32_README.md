## 2. PLATER — The Same Omelette

The visual architecture can be summarised as follows:

```text
SAUCER
→ NETWORK
→ AI
→ HUMAN DECISION
→ TERRITORY
```

The upper layer calculates.

The lower layer absorbs the material consequences.

> **Benefits above. Costs below.**

The alternative order is:

```text
TERRITORY
→ COMMUNITY
→ AI
→ NETWORK
```

In this order:

- the territory establishes limits;
- the community deliberates;
- AI helps read;
- the network coordinates.

The collective 1 appears when dispersed human positions become a shared capacity to change direction.

---

## 3. It is not “renewables: yes or no”

The prior question is:

> **From where is the transition calculated, who decides its direction, and who absorbs its material costs?**

The image summarises an inverted architecture:

- forecasting is produced above;
- forecasts become targets, power requirements, and surface allocations;
- the territory enters later as the location of implementation;
- and territorial actors are left to correct the model afterwards through objections and claims.

The issue is not whether renewable energy should exist.

The issue is the architecture through which the transition is calculated, decided, implemented, and territorially verified.

---

## 4. Inverted order

```text
SAUCER
> NETWORK
> AI
> DECISION
> TERRITORY
```

The process begins with:

- future demand;
- aggregated targets;
- expected power;
- and surfaces identified as available.

The calculation then selects where implementation can occur.

Communities receive the transformation and must later demonstrate what the model did not see:

- pre-existing territorial burdens;
- biodiversity;
- landscape;
- agriculture;
- water;
- habitability;
- and accumulated limits.

The territory is treated as a surface available for deployment before being recognised as a living, inhabited, productive, ecological, and historical system.

---

## 5. Territorial order

```text
TERRITORY
> COMMUNITY
> AI
> NETWORK
```

First, the real conditions and functions of the territory are recognised.

Then:

1. people and institutions deliberate;
2. AI helps compare alternatives;
3. the network coordinates implementation;
4. the result returns capacity to the territory.

The network does not merely extract:

- land;
- resources;
- infrastructure capacity;
- attention;
- and decision-making margin.

It must coordinate a transition that remains compatible with the material base that sustains it.

---

## 6. Core territorial critique

The critique does not reject the energy transition.

It questions a transition that:

- keeps the geography of consumption intact;
- while mainly transforming the geography of those who produce;
- cultivate;
- steward;
- and live on the land.

A transition cannot be considered territorially just merely because the technology is classified as renewable.

Its justice also depends on:

- where infrastructures are placed;
- who makes the decision;
- which cumulative burdens are recognised;
- who receives the benefits;
- who absorbs the costs;
- what functions are displaced;
- and what capacity returns to the territory.

---

## 7. The omelette does not turn itself

An individual can:

- observe;
- document;
- compare;
- submit formal objections;
- and warn.

But changing a territorial architecture requires many dispersed positions to become a collective capacity for orientation.

> **One person can recognise the problem.  
> The collective 1 can change the direction.**

---

## 8. The four movements of the collective 1

### 1. Situated signal

The territory and those who inhabit it detect:

- contradictions;
- pressures;
- cumulative burdens;
- and limits that do not appear in the aggregated view.

The signal begins in a specific place and condition.

It does not claim to explain the whole system by itself.

---

### 2. Common reading

Municipalities, citizens, organisations, and communities share information.

They distinguish:

- verified facts;
- hypotheses;
- uncertainties;
- and open questions.

The signal ceases to be isolated and becomes a common field of reading.

---

### 3. Deliberation

Individual positions are organised into:

- criteria;
- objections;
- distributed proposals;
- shared priorities;
- and coordinated institutional action.

Deliberation does not erase differences.

It gives them a structure capable of producing orientation.

---

### 4. Change of direction

The collective 1 can:

- introduce common limits;
- demand cumulative assessment;
- require territorial alternatives;
- and ensure that the plan adapts to the territory rather than forcing the territory to adapt to the plan.

The collective capacity does not merely react to implementation.

It modifies the order in which the decision is produced.

---

## 9. The same omelette

> **The same omelette. But now the actor capable of turning it becomes visible: the collective 1.**

The collective 1 is not:

- a leader;
- an expert above everyone else;
- a single organisation;
- or a symbolic saviour.

It is the organised capacity to transform dispersed signals into common orientation.

Its function is:

```text
SITUATED SIGNAL
→ COMMON READING
→ DELIBERATION
→ CHANGE OF DIRECTION
```

---

## 10. Changing direction

The collective 1 converts dispersed territorial signals into a shared capacity to:

- deliberate;
- establish limits;
- compare alternatives;
- require accountability;
- and organise the energy transition from the real conditions of the territory.

The operational sequence is:

```text
TERRITORY
> COMMUNITY
> AI
> NETWORK
```

And its functions are:

```text
limits
· deliberation
· reading
· coordination
```

The direction changes when:

- the territory ceases to enter last;
- material limits enter before allocation;
- local knowledge is not treated as a late correction;
- and implementation is subordinated to territorial verification.

---

## 11. Territorial test

A transition organised from the territory should be able to answer:

1. Which territorial functions already exist?
2. Which burdens are already present?
3. Which impacts accumulate with other projects?
4. Which alternatives reduce transformation of productive and ecological land?
5. Which communities participate before targets become fixed locations?
6. What limits are binding?
7. What infrastructure is genuinely necessary?
8. What capacity returns to the territory?
9. Who is accountable if the projected balance fails?
10. Can the direction still be changed before the cost becomes irreversible?
