# 10 · APPLICATION, VERIFICATION AND CUSTODY PROTOCOL

**BIOIA / WUOM**  
**Directory:** `05_METAARCHITECTURE`  
**Status:** v0.1  
**Nature:** operational metaarchitecture protocol  
**Scope:** application, deliberation, verification, correction, suspension, stopping, registration and custody

---

## 1. Purpose

This protocol establishes the minimum procedure for applying, verifying, correcting, suspending, stopping and holding a BIOIA implementation in custody.

Its function is to put into practice what is defined in:

- the licence;
- the ethical code;
- the principles;
- the laws;
- the adoption and non-appropriation criteria;
- the operational grammar;
- the architecture of orientation and territorial return.

The protocol does not replace situated deliberation.

Nor does it create a closed central authority.

It establishes a public, verifiable and revisable framework to prevent an implementation from merely declaring coherence without demonstrating it.

---

## 2. Operational Thesis

A practice cannot be considered a BIOIA implementation merely because it:

- uses artificial intelligence;
- cites the principles;
- adopts the vocabulary;
- declares a social or environmental purpose;
- publishes a report;
- includes a symbolic consultation;
- promises future return.

It must demonstrate a complete sequence:

**Situated problem → identified territory → signals → experience → deliberation → orientation → proportional action → verification → territorial return → learning**

---

## 3. Prior Conditions

Before an implementation begins, the following must be identified:

1. the situated problem;
2. the affected territory;
3. the actors involved;
4. the people and communities potentially affected;
5. the explicit purpose;
6. the non-negotiable limits;
7. the resources required;
8. the infrastructure used;
9. the role of artificial intelligence;
10. the parties responsible for the decision;
11. the foreseeable costs;
12. the risks;
13. the expected territorial return;
14. the correction and stopping mechanisms.

If any of these conditions cannot be defined, the implementation must not proceed.

---

## 4. Phase 0 · Case Opening

Every implementation begins with an initial record.

### 4.1. Minimum Data

The record must state:

- case title;
- territory;
- opening date;
- responsible parties;
- participating actors;
- problem description;
- purpose;
- status;
- document version;
- applicable licence;
- level of public access.

### 4.2. Initial Status

The case must be marked as:

> Open · unresolved · under reading

A solution must not be presented before the territorial reading has been completed.

---

## 5. Phase 1 · Problem Definition

The problem must be formulated without reducing it to the available tool.

### 5.1. Mandatory Questions

- What is happening?
- Where is it happening?
- Since when?
- Who observes it?
- Who receives the impact?
- Which relations are compromised?
- Which limits are being exceeded?
- Which part of the problem is known?
- Which uncertainties remain?
- Which capacities already exist in the territory?

### 5.2. Prohibition of an Anticipated Solution

The process must not begin with formulations such as:

- we need artificial intelligence;
- we need a platform;
- we need to automate;
- we need to centralize data.

The correct formulation begins with the problem and the purpose.

---

## 6. Phase 2 · Territorial Reading

Territorial reading integrates:

- signals;
- experience;
- knowledge;
- actors;
- temporalities;
- relations;
- impacts;
- capacities;
- limits.

### 6.1. Signals

They may include:

- data;
- direct observation;
- memory;
- ecological changes;
- temperature;
- water;
- soil;
- biodiversity;
- infrastructure;
- health;
- mobility;
- public services;
- economic activity;
- conflicts;
- testimonies.

### 6.2. Minimum Layers

Where relevant, the reading must consider:

- ecological layer;
- social layer;
- economic layer;
- institutional layer;
- technological layer;
- historical layer;
- cultural layer;
- temporal layer;
- power layer;
- return layer.

### 6.3. Uncertainty

Uncertainty must be recorded.

It must not be concealed through:

- scores;
- models;
- averages;
- predictions;
- visualizations;
- technical language.

---

## 7. Phase 3 · Map of Actors and Responsibilities

Every implementation must identify:

- who observes;
- who contributes experience;
- who produces knowledge;
- who finances;
- who controls the infrastructure;
- who controls the data;
- who designs;
- who deliberates;
- who decides;
- who executes;
- who receives the benefit;
- who absorbs the cost;
- who verifies;
- who is accountable for harm;
- who receives the return.

### 7.1. Non-Delegable Responsibility

Responsibility cannot be attributed to:

- artificial intelligence;
- the algorithm;
- the platform;
- automation;
- technical complexity;
- the organization in the abstract.

An identifiable person, institution or responsible structure must exist.

---

## 8. Phase 4 · Assessment of Necessity and Proportionality

Before artificial intelligence is used, the following questions must be answered:

- Is it necessary?
- Which concrete problem does it address?
- Is there a simpler alternative?
- What scale is sufficient?
- Which model is appropriate?
- What data does it require?
- What material cost does it produce?
- Which human or institutional capacity may it displace?
- Can it operate locally?
- Can it be audited?
- Can it be stopped?
- Can it be maintained without excessive dependence?

### 8.1. Rule

> The chosen technological solution must not exceed the scale required to understand, orient or act upon the problem.

### 8.2. Possible Decision

The assessment may conclude that the appropriate course is to:

- use artificial intelligence;
- use a simpler tool;
- combine tools;
- continue observing;
- not intervene;
- stop an existing intervention.

---

## 9. Phase 5 · Deliberation

Deliberation must take place before the final decision.

### 9.1. Participants

Where relevant, it must include:

- affected people;
- communities;
- institutions;
- specialists;
- territorial custodians;
- technical responsible parties;
- public responsible parties;
- economic actors;
- independent observers.

### 9.2. Conditions

Deliberation must be:

- informed;
- understandable;
- accessible;
- documented;
- open to objections;
- free from manipulation;
- proportional to the impact;
- linked to the decision.

### 9.3. Non-Symbolic Participation

Real deliberation does not exist when:

- the decision has already been made;
- the consultation cannot modify the project;
- key information is hidden;
- language prevents understanding;
- objections are not recorded;
- the community appears only as a legitimizing image.

---

## 10. Phase 6 · Orientation

Orientation defines:

- the purpose;
- the limits;
- the direction of action;
- exclusions;
- responsibilities;
- expected return;
- review conditions.

### 10.1. Possible Orientations

Orientation may decide to:

- act;
- limit;
- reduce;
- restore;
- protect;
- adapt;
- wait;
- observe;
- correct;
- suspend;
- stop;
- not deploy technology.

### 10.2. Rule

> Orientation precedes action.

---

## 11. Phase 7 · Action Design

Every action must describe:

- objective;
- scope;
- duration;
- territory;
- responsible parties;
- infrastructure;
- data;
- costs;
- risks;
- indicators;
- review mechanisms;
- territorial return;
- suspension conditions;
- closure conditions.

### 11.1. Explicit Limits

The following must be established:

- permitted uses;
- excluded uses;
- prohibited data;
- maximum duration;
- geographical scope;
- authorized actors;
- access capacity;
- reuse conditions;
- automation limits.

---

## 12. Phase 8 · Controlled Implementation

The implementation must begin, whenever possible, at a limited scale.

### 12.1. Principle of Minimum Intervention

Preference must be given to:

- the smallest sufficient scope;
- the least sufficient infrastructure;
- the smallest sufficient volume of data;
- the least sufficient automation;
- the lowest sufficient consumption;
- the greatest possible reversibility.

### 12.2. Prohibition of Automatic Expansion

Initial positive results do not authorize immediate expansion.

Every extension requires:

- a new reading;
- new deliberation;
- a new proportionality assessment;
- new cost verification;
- a renewed commitment to return.

---

## 13. Phase 9 · Verification

Verification is not limited to checking whether the tool works.

It must verify:

- whether the purpose is maintained;
- whether the limits are respected;
- whether responsibility remains identified;
- whether costs correspond to what was expected;
- whether unanticipated harms appear;
- whether capacities are displaced;
- whether benefit is concentrated;
- whether the territory receives return;
- whether the system remains proportional.

### 13.1. Territorial Verification

The territory must be able to show:

- changes;
- impacts;
- improvements;
- harms;
- limits;
- returns;
- absences of return.

### 13.2. Sources of Verification

They may include:

- observation;
- indicators;
- open data;
- testimonies;
- audits;
- community review;
- institutional review;
- independent assessment;
- technical documentation;
- temporal comparison.

---

## 14. Phase 10 · Territorial Return

Return must be defined from the beginning and verified during implementation.

### 14.1. Types of Return

It may consist of:

- accessible knowledge;
- tools;
- infrastructure;
- autonomy;
- public capacity;
- community capacity;
- improved services;
- ecological restoration;
- reduction of impacts;
- training;
- documentation;
- transfer of capacities;
- institutional continuity;
- ecological continuity.

### 14.2. The Following Are Not Sufficient Return

- publicity;
- reputation;
- a report without usable capacity;
- a donation disconnected from the impact;
- delayed compensation;
- a closed tool;
- a future promise;
- a non-verifiable metric;
- an action outside the affected territory.

### 14.3. Rule

> Without verifiable territorial return, there is no BIOIA closure.

---

## 15. Right to Object

Every affected person, community or institution must be able to submit objections.

### 15.1. An Objection May Concern

- purpose;
- data;
- consent;
- impact;
- opacity;
- proportionality;
- discrimination;
- absence of return;
- improper use of the name;
- concentration of power;
- breach of limits;
- territorial risk.

### 15.2. Treatment

Every objection must:

- be recorded;
- receive a response;
- be assessed;
- produce correction where appropriate;
- remain traceable.

---

## 16. Correction

An implementation must be corrected when:

- unanticipated impacts appear;
- the approved scale is exceeded;
- limits are breached;
- the purpose changes;
- costs are concealed;
- deliberation is reduced;
- artificial intelligence replaces decisions;
- return does not occur;
- responsibility becomes diluted;
- the territory shows deterioration.

### 16.1. Types of Correction

- technical modification;
- reduction of scope;
- change of data;
- change of responsible party;
- expansion of deliberation;
- revision of limits;
- redistribution of benefits;
- reinforcement of return;
- temporary suspension.

---

## 17. Suspension

An implementation must be suspended when:

- reasonable indications of harm exist;
- operation cannot be verified;
- responsibility is unclear;
- unauthorized uses appear;
- human control is lost;
- the licence is breached;
- appropriation occurs;
- auditing is blocked;
- return disappears;
- the purpose changes without deliberation.

Suspension does not necessarily mean permanent closure.

It permits:

- investigation;
- documentation;
- correction;
- deliberation;
- reassessment.

---

## 18. Stopping

An implementation must be stopped when it:

- causes serious or irreversible harm;
- violates non-negotiable limits;
- replaces deliberation and decision-making;
- uses incompatible surveillance or manipulation;
- extracts data without return;
- cannot be audited;
- cannot be corrected;
- depends on concealing impacts in order to continue;
- privatizes benefit and externalizes cost;
- loses coherence with the BIOIA core.

### 18.1. Rule

> A practice that cannot be stopped cannot be considered oriented.

---

## 19. Withdrawal of the BIOIA Designation

When a practice ceases to meet the criteria, it must:

- cease presenting itself as a BIOIA implementation;
- record the reason;
- preserve traceability;
- distinguish reused material from the implementation;
- inform the affected parties;
- document closure measures.

Withdrawal of the name does not remove responsibility for impacts already produced.

---

## 20. Public Record

Every implementation must maintain a record containing:

- version;
- date;
- responsible parties;
- purpose;
- territory;
- actors;
- infrastructure;
- data;
- decisions;
- objections;
- corrections;
- suspensions;
- verifications;
- return;
- final status.

### 20.1. Possible Statuses

- open;
- under reading;
- under deliberation;
- oriented;
- under implementation;
- under verification;
- corrected;
- suspended;
- stopped;
- closed;
- in custody.

---

## 21. Custody

Custody ensures that:

- the core remains visible;
- documents remain accessible;
- versions can be compared;
- modifications are recorded;
- objections are not erased;
- adaptation is not confused with appropriation;
- return can be verified;
- the memory of the process remains available.

Custody is not equivalent to absolute central control.

It is a responsibility for continuity, traceability and protection of coherence.

---

## 22. Distributed Custody

Custody may be shared among:

- territory;
- communities;
- institutions;
- technical responsible parties;
- public archives;
- repositories;
- observers;
- situated nodes.

The following must be avoided:

- dependence on a single person;
- concentration of keys;
- documentary loss;
- subsequent closure;
- private capture;
- modification without history.

---

## 23. Continuity and Succession of Custody

Every public architecture must anticipate what happens if its initiator ceases to maintain it.

There must be:

- sufficient documentation;
- a clear licence;
- an accessible repository;
- version history;
- continuity criteria;
- a copying mechanism;
- identification of possible custodians;
- prohibition of unilateral privatization of the public core.

### 23.1. Principle

> The continuity of BIOIA must not depend exclusively on the continuity of one person.

---

## 24. Closure of an Implementation

An implementation may be closed when:

- the purpose has been achieved;
- return has been verified;
- impacts have been documented;
- responsibilities have been concluded;
- data have a defined destination;
- infrastructure has a withdrawal or continuity plan;
- objections have been answered;
- learning has been recorded.

### 24.1. Closure Status

Closure must indicate:

- result;
- return;
- limits;
- harms;
- learning;
- pending elements;
- conditions for reopening.

---

## 25. Reopening

A closed case may be reopened when:

- new signals appear;
- the territory changes;
- delayed impacts appear;
- infrastructure changes;
- the purpose is modified;
- return ceases to be sustained;
- new actors submit justified objections.

Custody allows reopening without beginning from zero.

---

## 26. Minimum Checklist

Before an implementation is approved, the following questions must be answered affirmatively:

- Is the problem situated?
- Is the territory identified?
- Is the purpose explicit?
- Are the limits defined?
- Does experience participate?
- Can deliberation modify the decision?
- Is artificial intelligence necessary?
- Is the scale proportional?
- Are responsible parties identified?
- Are costs visible?
- Are risks documented?
- Is there a right to object?
- Can it be corrected?
- Can it be suspended?
- Can it be stopped?
- Is there territorial return?
- Can the return be verified?
- Is the documentation public?
- Is custody anticipated?
- Can the BIOIA designation be withdrawn if coherence is lost?

---

## 27. Summary Flow

**Opening → reading → actor map → proportionality → deliberation → orientation → limited implementation → verification → return → correction or closure → custody**

---

## 28. Synthesis Formulas

### Application

**Do not begin with the tool. Begin with the situated problem.**

### Verification

**Do not check only whether it works. Check for whom, at what cost and with what return.**

### Correction

**Every implementation must be able to revise its orientation.**

### Stopping

**Every implementation must be able to stop.**

### Custody

**Openness is protected through traceability, memory and distributed responsibility.**

### Closing

**Without verification, correction, capacity to stop and territorial return, there is no complete BIOIA implementation.**
